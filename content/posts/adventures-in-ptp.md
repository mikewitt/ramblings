When I first started looking, I noticed a distinct trend in information on PTP. Personally, I think that the general consensus on PTP is wrong. Namely, PTP isn't that complicated to get setup, it doesn't *require* a hardware clock, nor does it require any specific hardware support. If you want to get to sub-microsecond precision, some of those things may become important, but a basic setup doesn't require that. Of course, if you want to get down to the advertised nanosecond-level (or in the case of White Rabbit extensions, 100s of picoseconds) precision, then you probably do need some of that, but not all of it. 

## A Modest Proposal
One goal I ended up having in my homelab was to have a reliable source of time for everything. My goal was primarily to force everything to sync to my time server locally, and not hit the web. This would reduce an attack surface, but it should make everything a good deal more precise. The easy way started as using my firewall to sync to an NTP server on the web, and set up a rule{{% sidenote %}} which I didn't really do{{% /sidenote %}}to make it so that NTP requests would be silently redirected to the firewall and not sent out over the internet. This is supposed to be relatively trivial, but of course I had to make things difficult.

I've been aware for a while that you could hook up a GPS receiver and use that as a time source for an NTP server, which would increase the accuracy of the local server. Of course, if it's worth doing it's worth overdoing so I instead started to look into the possibility of having a dedicated appliance to do this for me. One thing led to another and of course 'if something is worth doing it's worth overdoing' and now I have an Ovenized Crystal Oscillator that's GPS disciplined, and a GPS antenna bolted to the side of my house.{{% sidenote %}}Which is also a fun conversation starter for folks that might want to know *why* I would want a GPS antenna on an immovable object{{% /sidenote %}} In any event, I bought a piece of equipment off eBay, and like any good eBay purchase, I had little idea *what* I actually bought.

However, what I did know right off the bat was that the GPS wouldn't work properly--it suffers from something called 'week number rollover' which is a result of the GPS week number being only represented by a 10-bit number that therefore rolls over every 1024 weeks or ~19 years. I also knew that people had the firmware updates available, along with [instructions on getting the NTP server that's built in to work](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/testgear/symmetricom-xli-license-hack/). My version included a card that can output IEEE 1588, or PTP. The model number of that card indicated that it *should* be PTPv2, or IEEE 1588-2008 compatible.

In my blissful ignorance, I assumed I could get it working, and worst case, I'd be able to synchronize something via PTP to serve NTP time. Now, at this point, I found varying levels of unhelpful information suggesting that what I wanted to do was overkill, or weird, or that PTP doesn't really make sense to do here. I also found some warnings that PTPv1 was not compatible with PTPv2{{% sidenote %}} *foreshadowing*{{% /sidenote %}}, but I figured I'd work around that if I had to.

## The eBay Gamble
Before and after ordering, I spent days poring over datasheets about the Symmetricom XLi I had purchased. Little did I realize, but the PTP Grandmaster{{% sidenote %}}'Master', 'Slave', and 'Grandmaster' are terms used in the 1588-2002 and 1588-2008 spec, althgouh I don't know about the 2019 update. For the sake of keeping my sanity, I am going to stick with these terms, although I realize that some find it offensive.{{% /sidenote %}} version of the XLi is different from the regular XLi. In reading, I discovered a fair amount of information on these, although not quite what I was looking for. I found an [archive] of firmware that had various versions of the XLi firmware, archived from when said firmware was distributed freely on the Symmetricom website (Symmetricom has since been purchased by Microchip, and seems to have completely discontinued firmware for these). 

Finally, after getting the XLi, plugging it in, and checking out what I had *actually* bought and not what I had *thought* I bought, I started to look at what I could do. The XLi I purchased did not have the NTP license, so I couldn't just use it out of the box to serve time. It also suffered (as expected) from the WNRO bug and thought it was sometime in 2006 (I think?). However, things started to unravel from here. Despite having the right card for PTPv2 (part number 089-00152-000), it showed as PTPv1 (really, as 'PTP' because it's not like they predicted being 'obsolete' that soon after release). The card also showed in the web interface as being 87-8036, which is the P/N for the PTPv1 card. 

Doing some digging, I realized my XLi was running firmware 1.92. From my research I had found out that 2.40 was the most recent (and last) firmware revision for the XLi. But, I went into the [archive] I found and realized that despite multiple versions of firmware from 1.80 to 2.40, the firmware was discontinuous in numbering... and didn't include a 1.92. This is when I started to think about something. The commands for controlling the PTP clock seemed specific. And, I was (and am still) guessing that the commands for the XLi firmware are tied to the firmware itself. In fact, some of the commands that were 'present' in my firmware were 'unavailable'--and while they related to cards I didn't have, the manual also seemed to indicate that they weren't *usable*. So, there was a distinct possibiltiy that if I upgrade to 2.40, I may lose control over the PTP card... and without a backup in place, I can't revert to a firmware that works.{{% sidenote %}} A poster by the name of Alex Forencich had successfully modified the [firmware to fix the WNRO bug](https://lists.ctbirding.org/empathy/thread/UXOVZ2E4YGGNPSXLJSELLKOYGRB4ENPZ) (but didn't post it), but that indicated to me that the code wasn't signed, and there shouldn't be any specific restrictions on the firmware and upgrade/downgrade at will should be possible. {{% /sidenote %}} So, now it's become imperative that I somehow figure out how to use what I have, unless I want to give up on the PTP altogether. However, PTP should provide a higher level of accuracy than NTP.

From what I can tell, the PTPv1 spec was abandoned in favor of the new version for a few reasons, but accuracy wasn't one of them. I have a managed switch, and I can set up a dedicated VLAN for PTP, and make sure that the multicast packets make it everywhere. I can also control the priority of the packets on said VLAN, so it seems to me that for my use case, PTPv1 will be just as good as PTPv2 would be, although I need to get it working first...

## Software
Admittedly, I (kind of) saw this coming, and had asked Google's AI if and how to use existing software to sync the clock. Gemini helpfully told me that although the protocols are incompatible, the software `ptp4l` should have an appropriate mode to sync to a PTPv1 network. Spoiler alert: [it doesn't](https://linuxptp.nwtime.org/documentation/ptp4l/).

After doing some of my own thinking, I found the `ptpd` project. In the documentation, [it clearly states that it's for the PTPv2 protocol.](https://github.com/ptpd/ptpd#:~:text=PTP%20daemon%20(PTPd)%20is%20an%20implementation%20the%20Precision%20Time%20Protocol%20(PTP)%20version%202%20as%20defined%20by%20%27IEEE%20Std%201588%2D2008%27.) However, `ptpd` has been around since about 2005, before PTPv2. I found the old code, at least from [v1.1.0 of `ptpd`](https://github.com/ptpd/ptpd/tree/ptpd-1.1.0) which does support PTPv1, (and is the last to do so). It's also simple, and runs in software only--no reliance on hardware timestamping. I was able to compile that version with no (real) issues, and get it started. But... it still didn't work quite right. It also uses a PI controller to adjust the time,{{% sidenote %}}Using a PI controller isn't inherently a problem, but it does mean that it is by default not optimal, and probably not an unbiased estimator either. It makes my brain itch in the wrong way{{% /sidenote %}} and adjusts the system clock directly. The problem then becomes that I can no longer leverage another package like `chrony` to serve NTP time on the network, or I could but then not use the clock servo mechanism intrinsic to `chrony` which I assumed would be more sophisticated.

Of course, the code for `ptpd` v1.1.0 is written in C, and is fairly small. Conveniently, there is also a paper provided in there, which forms the basis of their implementation. In large part, the folks that made `ptpd`, at least initially, were a group of college researchers, and tested their sync implementation against the clock itself, and found that [they could synchronize to within a few microseconds,](https://github.com/ptpd/ptpd/blob/15d6c4ff892d2086590e08a2bdbcb3675a23137a/doc/ptpd_2005_1588_conference_paper.pdf) using only the software implementation. It does include a minor modification (which now appears standard) such that the kernel timestamps the packet so as to keep interrupt latency to a minimum, but nevertheless doesn't use hardware.

I realized I needed to modify the code to make it work with another package, such as `chrony`. Then, I needed to decide if it was worth it to (re)learn C to modify a 10+ year old program... and decided that the best course of action would be to learn a different language on my list: Rust.{{% sidenote %}} Obviously. Wouldn't *anyone* do the same? {{% /sidenote %}} In all seriousness, I thought first about writing it in Python, but Python is notoriously slow, which makes it the antithesis of what I should actually use here. The marshaled wire format of the PTP packets is simple, and the PTPv1 protocol is particularly so. I figured Rust was actually a reasonable choice,{{% sidenote %}}or I suppose I should say, *would* be reasonable if I already knew Rust{{% /sidenote %}} although I realize there is some irony in writing a program to interface a protocol that was obsolete well before the first iteration of that programming language was conceived.

At this point, my 'simple' task of getting the Symmetricom XLi I had purchased working as a local time source had now turned into a project to write software to interface with the time server, synchronize time to it, and then pass that along. More or less (mostly less) I've gotten the software written, and capable of measuring the timestamps necessary for PTP. I still have to figure out how to properly synchronize the clocks, but that's where I am for now. 

## *More* Scope Creep
Of course, as all scope creep happens it crept more. Since I was now implementing software timestamping in Rust to enable me to synchronize the clock, it started to make me wonder what 'hardware' timestamping entailed, and if it would be possible to do. I had an extra Raspberry Pi 5 which I had dedicated to this project. And in digging, I found out that the NIC chipset did in fact have a physical hardware clock, for timestamping PTP packets. Now, remember where PTPv1 and PTPv2 are not compatible? It's worth noting that my assumption about hardware timestamping, from everything I could tell, was that there was/is a hardware clock running on the NIC. When enabled, it can add metadata to PTP packets with time information, or add the time on transmit. Especially, on transmit, this would allow the NIC to fill in the blanks where the time should be. But, since PTPv1 and PTPv2 are not compatible, I thought that this might be a fools errand. 

I quickly came to realize that it (probably) isn't! Those NIC PHCs can work in a few different ways. Some of them are the more direct version and are capable of stamping the time in the packet as it is transmitted, but for the most part, the expectation would be that you'd pass the packet to the NIC along with the expected transmit time and it will queue it to transmit at that time, rather than trying to timestamp it. Additionally, I came to realize that outside of synchronizing the PHC to the PTP system, you wouldn't gain much without the ability to do *other* things with that PHC. Examples of things like 'high frequency trading' and audio sync aren't as useful if you don't also know the usable timestamps. 

Finally, I also came to realize that some of the PTP spec is a bit of a sham. The official spec is more or less rather simple. The *spec* says:
 - Master transmit at time T1 (SYNC)
 - Optionally, follow-up with a more precise timestamp T1 (FOLLOW_UP)
 - Slave receive packet at T2'
 - Slave may request delay measurement (DELAY_REQ)
 - DELAY_REQ sent at time T3', which is in the packet
 - Master receives at time T4
 - Master responds with time T4 (DELAY_RESP)

But, looking more closely, the Master doesn't care about T3'. And, outside of T3', the master doesn't know about T2' and wouldn't be able to generate its own delay estimate. Furthermore, by design, T2' and T3' are meant to be corrupted estimates of T2 and T3.[^1]

I've come to realize that you can ascertain the actual time that a packet was sent via drivers. Which also makes the FOLLOW_UP packet make more sense. Ultimately the PHC never actually needs to modify the packet. For the most part, this is similar to PTPv2. However, PTPv2 introduces an additional clock type, a 'transparent clock'. A transparent clock **must** add in timestamps to a packet to work, because it needs to be accurate, and do so at a low level. I think there ends up being some confustion online because a transparent clock has to work the way that I initially thought the PHC worked... but the PHC can be (and is) much simpler.

All of this is to say that despite not supporting PTPv1 in hardware, the PHC itself should work just fine with the older PTPv1 spec. And, we don't need to deal with the unicast things in PTPv2 (which was a driving factor apparently in designing PTPv1). So, in many ways this is much easier than if we were doing this for PTPv2 (ignoring, of course, the fact that if we were PTPv2 compliant that I wouldn't need to do any of this).{{% sidenote %}}I'm pretty sure this is called ['denial'](https://www.hcf.com.au/health-agenda/body-mind/mental-health/moving-through-grief){{% /sidenote %}}
